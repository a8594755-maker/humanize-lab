Irene Rosenfeld at Mondelez International.

Diversification. Kraft grew its snack business through Mergers and Acquisitions. Buying other food companies made these deals examples of Horizontal Integration. In 2007, Kraft paid Danone $7.2 billion for LU biscuits. With LU's distribution network already in place in Asia, Kraft could sell Oreo in more places. In 2010, Kraft paid about $19 billion for Cadbury. The purchase brought in chocolate and gum, plus candy brands and helped Kraft grow in India. Later, Mondelez bought Kinh Do in Vietnam and Enjoy Life Foods, a maker of allergy-friendly snacks. Since these purchases kept the company in the food business, they counted as Related Diversification.

For one Strategic Alliance, Mondelez worked with Alibaba to sell snacks online in China. The two had a partnership, but it wasn't Horizontal Integration since Alibaba wasn't a snack company. In 2015, Mondelez joined its coffee business with D.E. Master Blenders' coffee business to form JDE. Since both companies sold coffee, the deal counted as both a Joint Venture and Horizontal Integration. Mondelez held on to 44% of JDE and got $4.24 billion from the deal. This looks like an Equity Strategic Alliance, though the case gives no separate example of one. Internal Development took the form of Mondelez creating Vea and Good Thins and taking Milka chocolate to China. It built a new factory.

Over time, Kraft shifted from big Acquisitions toward selling its snack brands in more countries. In China, for instance, Mondelez sold Oreo in small packs that suited local buying habits. Shoppers wanted products that were easy to use or healthier. Snacks were outpacing many grocery foods in growth. Mondelez still bought smaller businesses when those purchases fit its plans.

Reversing Diversification. Kraft's sale of its pizza brands to Nestle in 2010 was an example of Divestment. Some of that money helped pay for Cadbury too. By splitting off Kraft Foods Group in 2012, Kraft made it a company of its own. This was the biggest example of Spin-offs. North American grocery brands such as Jell-O and Oscar Mayer stayed with Kraft Foods Group. Snack brands such as Oreo and Cadbury, plus Ritz stayed with Mondelez. Keeping costs down mattered for the slower-growing grocery business, whereas reaching new markets meant spending more on the snack business. Splitting the two made sense for that reason.

In 2015, Mondelez put its coffee business into JDE. Keeping a share of JDE let it spend more time on snacks without dealing directly with shifts in coffee prices. As a form of Retrenchment, Mondelez cut spending through zero-based budgeting. Between 2010 and 2016, it also cut its factory count from 170 to 130 and made fewer kinds of products. Alongside those cuts, it built new factories and upgraded old ones to make snacks more efficiently.

Restructuring changed day-to-day work too. To save money, Mondelez brought office services such as finance and human resources together. In 2016, it gave local managers more say and shifted some work from global teams to regional teams. Slower growth in China and other emerging markets in 2013 made these changes matter more. Investors such as Nelson Peltz wanted better profit margins as well.

Recommendations. I think Mondelez should stick with Related Diversification and Internal Development to make more healthy snacks. Smaller Acquisitions could help too, as long as the brands fit. I'd stay away from buying businesses that are too different. The BCG Matrix could help sort out where the money should go. I'd put more money into Stars, use Cash Cows to back new products and weigh Question Marks carefully. Think about selling dogs. Mondelez should save where it can, in my view, without cutting too deeply into marketing or product quality.
